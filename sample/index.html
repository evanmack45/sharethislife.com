We've Been in Woody Ever Since

Alda Weringer, in her own words.

I was born in California, that's Calaveras County, on October the 19th, 1897.

I was six months old when my folks left Angel's Camp and they moved to Bodie. Then I lived there until I was about five, and then we came to Nevada. My father had relatives. He was born in Carson City, Nevada. So we left Bodie — all the mines had shut down up there, he happened to be a miner — to Carson City. And from there, we moved to Virginia City, and we lived in several little mining towns. We lived in Virginia City, Gold Hill, Silver City, and he worked in Tonopah. All of the family moved over there. And then we lived in Gold Hill the last eight or nine years. All together we lived in Nevada for fourteen years. And then we came to California. We lived in Bakersfield.

He and my mother, they got my father out of their hot mines and got him to come to Bakersfield. And when we got here to Bakersfield, well, we heard about this mine, this copper mine up at Woody, and he headed for that, and I've been in Woody ever since. That was in 1916. We came to Bakersfield on the first day of October in 1916.

My father was a miner, you know. He did timbering in the shafts. He was a very good timberman, and he evidently knew a lot about surveying and things like that. When he wasn't doing that, well, he was either a farmer or a boss of the mines. And the mines were those very deep, hot mines, a mile deep somewhere, and they'd work ten minutes, and then they'd have to jump in a tub of a great big vat of cold water and cool off. Oh, I don't know, because it wasn't a very nice job, and I mean, it was a dangerous job and everything. So my mother was afraid. He was a young man then, and she was afraid that he was going to get the miners' consumption. [Unclear: she says something about "Jim and his father" talking to him; the names are not clear on the recording.]

And then he worked in Woody at the old copper mine, which then belonged to Joseph Warren. And he had a son, Francis Joseph [Weringer — the transcript also has "Waringer"; the archive spells the family name Weringer], and that's who I married, and I was there ever since. And I wouldn't leave it, but my husband passed away two years ago, and I have three children. Marjorie Dillette, and Joseph Weringer Jr., and Richard Weringer. [Spelling of "Dillette" is as it came out of the transcript and should be checked.]

My father stayed in [Woody — the transcript has "Moody"] before [unclear mine name, heard as "Rosloat"] Mine shut down. It was a copper mine, and no electricity in that country at that time, and they had to generate their own electricity in these big, these great big engines, 400 horsepower engines that they had to generate it with. Well, it just took all the money that these men that put up the money, that they called stockholders, [had]. So they had to shut it down.

Then my father came after that shutdown, then he came to Bakersfield, back to Bakersfield. He worked for the Santa Fe Railroad, and he went to work today, and he worked there for quite a long time, because he passed away when he was 64 years old. I don't know what year that was now. Well, it was 30— no, it wasn't 40. It was in the 30s. 38, yes. 38. Anyhow, my mother stayed in Bakersfield.

When we came here, Bakersfield wasn't a very big city at that time. A lot of the streets weren't yet paved, and you know a lot of things. I know that the old brewery that was in Bakersfield before the turn of the century was owned by husband's mother, and she, her name was Miller at that time, and she owned this brewery. Her husband had passed away and she had nine children. And my husband's father — he was born in Vienna, Austria, and he came to Bakersfield as a young man, and, I forget how, well, he went to work in this brewery as a bartender, and after her husband died, he kept on tending the bar, and then he married her, and after they were married, then from that union, my husband and his sister were born. See, then the name was Weringer, because before it was yours. [The last clause is unclear on the recording; it may be the interviewer speaking.]

Oh, and they had a big fire in Bakersfield one time, and the fire come right up to the fence of the brewery, and they put the fire out with beer. They didn't have a lot of water. I don't really know what that date was, but it had to be in the [unclear: heard as "in the week"]. My husband was born in 1886, and he was a good-sized kid, I think, when this happened. So, if it was in the 1880s, I would kind of say that that's when it was.

It was called the Weringer Brewery. No, not the Weringer, because it was new at first. I don't know if I ever heard of anything but just the old brewery. They called it the Bakersfield Brewery. In fact, it was the only one there at that time. That's some of the old tales that he told me, see, and everything else.

And the old Scribner Opera House was here at that time. Used to have all these, what they called, these plays that come through all the time. They called it vaudeville. Touring? Yes, uh-huh. And they'd come through here. You had to come in an old Model T Ford or horse and buggy. There wasn't any real good cars.

I don't recall when the Opera House opened or when it was closed. But the location — well, yes, it was right there on Chester Avenue. And right across the street from it was [unclear: heard as "Broggs' Theater"]. That was on Nickelodeon, I think, in the movie picture place. It was right close to where the old J.C. Penney place was, before they moved, just across the alley, I think. I don't know the exact number, but it was on Chester, I confirmed that.

As for the timberman work, I was a pretty small kid, and all I know is that as they went down in the mines, they had the timber, you know, to keep the dirt from falling in. And it had to be exact, very exact. And he helped do some of the timbering when he came up there to [unclear: heard as "Ruby"]. They built another new shaft, and he and my husband together — that was before we were [married; the sentence breaks off] — surveyed it. They went in another hole and tunneled in and came up to meet the perpendicular shaft, and when that thing broke through, it was perfect. And that all made my father feel so good, because I can remember. They were all hilarious about it.

And we lived in tents up there. There was about 60 men working in this copper mine, and they didn't have any buildings, you know. They had the old hotel — this is the Woody store, not [what the interviewer called] the Haveler store — and a boarding house, because the men ate there, and then they had a few rooms in it. And then they built these tent houses. They were real nice, you know what I mean. They had a floor in them and then the tent on it, and it was boarded up this far, and then we had a fly over the top of it to keep the rain and the snow coming in, and they were really cozy and warm. And my mother and I, we fixed them all up like dollhouses where we lived.

And so then we didn't live there. Then the next year, after we came there, my husband and I took them in, and that was when everything began to kind of fall through, because they didn't have the money to go on with it, see? Being a copper mine, you know, copper wasn't too — during the war, after that, the copper went up in price. That was too late then for that. So then, after that, and all the people moved away, and there was no more work, see? So Woody kind of was just a little ghost town.

It was during the First World War. Well, 1916, that was the first World War, sure it was. And so then all those people moved away, and a lot of those men went to the war. And we got cards from a few of them over there in Germany, you know.

And so there were a lot of ranches off, you know, at distances, maybe two or three miles or maybe eight miles. And of course those ranchers stayed there because land was cheap in those days. They bought a lot of railroad land and things like that. The miners and the people who come in and worked in the mine, they all left. But they had a little school there, and they always had enough kids for school.

And now there's a post office. My husband was postmaster there for 42 years, and then when he retired I took it over and I was there 15 years. So we had it for about 57 years between us. And of course he had to work to make a living, because we got ten dollars a month to run the post office, you know. We only had mail every three times a week. I had three kids, pretty close — well, she was the middle one, and then the youngest boy, he was seven years later. When you have youngsters, you don't have that much wages. So we kept it all the time because he was very civic-minded, you know, and he just wouldn't part with that post office no matter what. So we just hung on to it, you know.

Well, now, I had to retire. I guess it's six years since he was gone. [She also said earlier that her husband passed away two years ago. Those two timings do not match, and should be checked.] And when I got out of there, my salary climbed up quite a bit, because we had an everyday mail, and registering and all things like that came along with it. I'd say it started up to be worthwhile in about, oh, maybe 1940. And of course the wage wasn't very high then either. After I retired, I often thought, gee, I wish I would have been getting them when I had — it was what she's getting now, you know, because my daddy wouldn't have had to work. It's so hard.

Then we accumulated some land, and we had about 300 acres of land. Then we had [unclear: heard as "a tea potter"]. After he passed away, we sold the cattle and I leased the land. And that's about up to date.

And Joe's father, my husband's father, he had a foresight that nobody could dream of, I don't believe. He had had it surveyed and lots and blocks and recreation areas and everything else. And that's in the county recorder's office. And somebody noticed it a while back, a couple of years ago. Somebody found that, and they started getting on, you know, they developed, developed, developed. So now it's in the process of developing, and I don't know what's going to come, but from his original plan he had the foresight that someday—

—The 18-minute clip ends here, mid-sentence.
